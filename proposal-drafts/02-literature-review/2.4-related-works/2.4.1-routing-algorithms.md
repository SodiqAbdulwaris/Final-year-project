### 2.4.1 Routing algorithms

Hybrid LLM predicts the quality gap between a smaller and a larger model and routes by a threshold, reducing calls to the large model by up to 40% in its tested setting (Ding et al., 2024). It uses a limited set of model pairs and says nothing about provider failure or changing load. RouteLLM also chooses between a stronger and a weaker model but learns from preference data and tests transfer to other model pairs; its Table 6 reports a 3.66× cost-saving ratio against GPT-4 at 95% of GPT-4’s MT-Bench quality, an operating-point-specific result (Ong et al., 2025).

RouterDC trains query and model representations with dual contrastive objectives and reports gains of 2.76% in distribution and 1.90% out of distribution (S. Chen et al., 2024). CSCR places prompts and models in a shared embedding space with cost-aware training and nearest-neighbour retrieval to cope with changing model pools (Shirkavand et al., 2025), and Shnitzer et al. (2023) show that benchmark correctness labels can train routers. These methods make cost–quality selection a sound starting point, but they do not show that the combination of task classification and embedding evidence proposed here will be better. A fair test must use the same requests and candidate deployments, include a simple baseline and count the cost of the router itself.
