## 1.1 Background

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used in applications that answer questions, summarise documents and assist with software development. This use is part of a wider rise in the adoption of artificial intelligence (AI). The 2025 Stanford AI Index reported that 78% of surveyed respondents said their organisations used AI in 2024, compared with 55% in 2023, and that the share reporting generative AI use in at least one business function rose from 33% to 71% over the same period (Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, 2025). These figures describe the organisations in the survey. They do not show what share of all organisations run LLMs in production.

Developers reach LLMs either through hosted application programming interfaces (APIs) or by running models on their own hardware. The models differ in price, response time and capability. One model may perform well on a hard task but cost much more per request, while another may be cheaper and adequate for easy tasks but unreliable on difficult ones. Chen et al. (2024) studied LLM APIs and found differences in both pricing and performance across providers, and they examined ways of choosing among models to manage this trade-off. Prices also change over time. The AI Index reported that the cost of querying a model that scored at GPT-3.5 level on the Massive Multitask Language Understanding (MMLU) benchmark fell from $20 per million tokens in November 2022 to $0.07 in October 2024 (Stanford Institute for Human-Centered Artificial Intelligence, 2025). That comparison holds at one benchmark score only. It does not mean the cheaper models are equally capable on every task.

A common practice is to send every request to one fixed model. This keeps configuration simple, but a short factual question and a long multi-step task receive the same treatment. Research on routing asks whether the model can instead be chosen for each request. RouteLLM, for example, uses human preference data to choose between a stronger and a weaker model, aiming to cut cost while keeping response quality acceptable (Ong et al., 2025). Its results show that routing can help under the conditions tested. They do not guarantee a benefit for every application.

Response time adds a further concern. A model that looks suitable on quality and price may be slow or overloaded when a request arrives. Da and Kalyvianaki (2026) studied model selection together with the load on the available model instances in a heterogeneous serving setting. They showed that quality, cost and latency interact, so a choice made without regard to the state of the deployment can cause delays. Their evaluation covers one serving environment and may not transfer to every mix of hosted and local models.

In Nigeria, these choices sit within a national effort to develop and use AI. The National Artificial Intelligence Strategy names AI infrastructure, adoption, responsible development and governance among its priorities (Federal Ministry of Communications, Innovation and Digital Economy, 2025). The strategy sets the policy context. It does not report how much Nigerian software teams spend on LLM services or what difficulties they meet most often.

Two Nigerian conditions make the choice of model more than a technical detail. The first is currency. Hosted LLM services are priced in US dollars, so a change in the naira–dollar exchange rate changes what a Nigerian team pays in naira even when the provider's dollar price stays the same. The African Development Bank (2025) linked the move to a floating exchange rate in June 2023 to continued depreciation of the naira [V3]. How large the effect is for a given application depends on its provider, usage and payment arrangements, and evidence from Nigerian organisations would be needed to measure it. The second is privacy. A request sent to a provider outside Nigeria may carry personal data, and sections 41 to 43 of the Nigeria Data Protection Act, 2023 set conditions for transferring such data abroad, including an adequate level of protection or another basis allowed by the Act (Nigeria Data Protection Act, 2023, ss. 41–43). Whether these sections apply depends on what data is sent, where it is processed and how the transfer is arranged. A local deployment can change where processing happens, but it does not remove the duty to protect personal data.

Availability and fit also matter. A hosted endpoint can fail or reject a request, and a local model depends on the hardware that runs it. An application tied to one endpoint cannot complete a request when that endpoint fails. If another deployment takes over, it must still meet the request's own limits. A substitute chosen only because it is available may break a budget, a capability need or a privacy restriction. These are operating conditions that must be tested alongside quality and cost.

Existing work supplies methods and benchmarks for parts of this problem. RouterBench stores model outputs and provides a framework for comparing routing strategies on cost and performance (Hu et al., 2024). Offline comparison of this kind lets different policies be tested on the same data. It does not reproduce the network delays, provider outages and failures an application meets in live operation.

The problem this project addresses is therefore how to choose, for each request, an LLM deployment that is permitted and suitable when quality, cost, latency and privacy requirements must be weighed together, and what should happen when the chosen deployment fails or no permitted alternative remains. The benefit and the overhead of any routing approach need to be measured against simpler choices under defined test conditions. They should not be assumed from the existence of several models.
